\section{Introduction}

This project predicts whether a credit-card client will have payment difficulty in the following month (\var{payment_difficulty_next_month}). The data come from the \emph{Default of Credit Card Clients} data set, with a semi-synthetic target. There are 25{,}000 labelled and 5{,}000 unlabelled clients, each described by 15 predictors: the credit limit, four demographic fields (recorded sex, education, marital status and age), eight summaries of the last six months of repayments, bills and payments, and whether the lender recorded a repayment-assistance plan. There are no missing values. Only 21.2\,\% of clients have payment difficulty, so a model that always answers ``no difficulty'' is already right 78.8\,\% of the time. Accuracy alone is therefore a poor guide.

For this reason we mainly compare models with the \textbf{AUC}. Each model gives every client a risk score between 0 and 1. The AUC answers a simple question: if we pick one client who had difficulty and one who did not, how often does the model give the higher score to the client who had difficulty? An AUC of 0.5 means the model is guessing, and 1.0 means it always ranks them correctly. The \textbf{ROC curve} is the picture behind this number: it shows how many clients in difficulty the model catches against how many false alarms it raises, for every possible cut-off on the score, and the AUC is the area under it. Because the AUC does not depend on any particular cut-off, we use it to compare and tune the models, and we choose the cut-off separately (Section~\ref{sec:thresholds}).

The goal is not only to predict well but also to understand what each model has learned and where their explanations disagree. We fit the three mandatory model families, a decision tree, logistic regression (LR) and an Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM), under the same protocol: the same preprocessing logic, the same five cross-validation (CV) folds for every decision, the same validation set and the same four clients for local explanations. We then compare their explanations both globally and for those individual clients.

The EBM turns out to be the best model. Its CV AUC of 0.776 is higher than that of LR (0.761) and the tree (0.752) in all five folds, and it even beats a black-box gradient-boosting model (0.768), so here interpretability costs nothing. The differences are modest, though, and the three models make the same decision for 87\,\% of the validation clients. All three agree that recent repayment behaviour drives the risk: the latest repayment status, the share of the bill that was paid, and the number of months with delays or with no payment. They disagree on which of these related variables carries the signal, and on the shape of the effects. The EBM finds clearly non-linear patterns, such as a U-shaped effect of the payment-to-bill ratio and of age, which LR cannot represent and the tree captures only roughly. These differences explain the clients on which the models contradict each other. We submit the EBM's predictions for \texttt{test.csv}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
The labelled data are split once into an 80\,\% \emph{training} set (20{,}000 rows) and a 20\,\% \emph{validation} set (5{,}000 rows), stratified by the target with seed 411. All decisions --- model comparison, hyperparameters, feature transformations and classification thresholds --- use the \emph{same} five stratified folds of the training set, as the assignment requires. Because all models share the folds, differences can be checked fold by fold (paired comparison). The validation set is used once, after every choice is fixed, to confirm the CV estimates and to supply unseen instances for local explanation. The unlabelled test set is never used for fitting or selection. The submitted model is refit with its fixed settings on all 25{,}000 labelled rows.

\subsection{Selection criteria and threshold choice}
\label{sec:thresholds}
Hyperparameters are chosen by \textbf{CV ROC AUC}. For the tree and LR we also apply the \textbf{one-standard-error rule}: among configurations whose CV AUC lies within one standard error of the best, we keep the simplest (the shallowest tree, then the largest leaves; the strongest regularization). In an interpretability project we prefer the smaller model whenever the data cannot distinguish the two.

\subsubsection{Thresholds}
For each model, every training client receives an \emph{out-of-fold} probability $\hat p_i$: the prediction of the model trained on the other four folds. For a threshold $t$ the predicted class is $\hat y_i(t)=\mathbb{1}[\hat p_i \geq t]$, which gives the counts TP$(t)$, TN$(t)$, FP$(t)$ and FN$(t)$ over all $n=20{,}000$ training clients. We evaluate
\[
\operatorname{Acc}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{TP}(t)+\mathrm{TN}(t)}{n},
\qquad
\operatorname{BA}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{TP}(t)}{\mathrm{TP}(t)+\mathrm{FN}(t)}+\frac{\mathrm{TN}(t)}{\mathrm{TN}(t)+\mathrm{FP}(t)}\right)
\]
on the grid $T=\{0.05, 0.06, \dots, 0.95\}$ and take
\[
t_{\text{acc}}=\arg\max_{t\in T}\operatorname{Acc}(t),
\qquad
t_{\text{bal}}=\arg\max_{t\in T}\operatorname{BA}(t).
\]
Balanced accuracy (BA) is the mean of the recall of each class, so both classes count equally whatever their size. $t_{\text{acc}}$ is used for the submitted test predictions, because the test set is graded on accuracy. $t_{\text{bal}}$ is our \emph{analysis operating point}, because missing a client in difficulty and raising a false alarm should both count. All class-specific results and local cases use $t_{\text{bal}}$.

\subsubsection{Why thresholds instead of class weights}
Class weights (e.g.\ \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"}) counter the imbalance during training by multiplying the loss of each positive client by about 3.7 ($=0.788/0.212$). We handle the imbalance with the threshold instead, for three reasons. First, it gives one mechanism for all three families. Reweighting acts differently on each model: in LR it mainly shifts the intercept, which is almost the same as lowering the threshold, while in the tree and the EBM it also changes which splits and shapes are learned. Moving only the threshold keeps the comparison about the models themselves.

Second, the probabilities keep their meaning. Without weights, a predicted 0.3 corresponds to roughly 30\,\% observed difficulty, and the model's base value (tree root, LR intercept, EBM intercept) reflects the real 21\,\% rate, so the local explanations in Section~\ref{sec:local} describe contributions to the real risk. Weighted models inflate all probabilities, and these numbers would lose that reading. Third, one fitted model serves both goals: the accuracy-graded submission ($t_{\text{acc}}$) and the balanced analysis ($t_{\text{bal}}$). Only $t$ changes, and $t$ itself is chosen by CV as the assignment requires.

\subsection{The three classifiers}
\subsubsection{Decision tree}
We use \texttt{DecisionTreeClassifier} (CART). Categorical predictors are one-hot encoded; numerical predictors keep their original units, because splits are invariant to monotone transformations and raw thresholds are easier to read. We searched \texttt{max\_depth} $\in\{2,\dots,8,10\}$ and \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $\in\{25,50,100,200,400\}$, the two parameters that directly control how many rules a reader must follow. CV AUC rises up to depth~5 and then plateaus (Figure~\ref{fig:performance}, right). The best configuration (depth~5, leaves $\geq 25$; 32 leaves; AUC 0.755) and the one-SE choice (depth~5, leaves $\geq 400$; \textbf{17 leaves}; AUC 0.752) are statistically indistinguishable, so we keep the tree with half as many leaves. Deeper trees add leaves without gaining AUC.

\subsubsection{Logistic regression}
We use \texttt{LogisticRegression} with an L2 penalty and the \texttt{lbfgs} solver. Numerical predictors are standardized, so coefficients are comparable: each coefficient is the change in log-odds per standard deviation. Categorical predictors use reference (drop-first) coding, which removes the perfect collinearity of full one-hot encoding with the intercept. The four monetary variables are strongly right-skewed. Whether to apply a signed $\log(1+|x|)$ transform to them was a \emph{feature-transformation choice inside the CV grid}, crossed with $C\in\{0.001,\dots,100\}$. The transform raises CV AUC from 0.750 to 0.762 at every $C$. The one-SE rule selects $C=0.01$ (AUC 0.761), where the best $C=0.1$ gives 0.762.

\subsubsection{Explainable Boosting Machine}
We use InterpretML's \texttt{ExplainableBoostingClassifier} with \texttt{interactions=0}, so the model is a pure generalized additive model: $\operatorname{logit} P(y=1\mid x)=\beta_0+\sum_j f_j(x_j)$. Unlike the other two models, the EBM receives the raw columns. It bins numerical variables itself and treats the string columns as nominal, so each original variable gets exactly one shape function~$f_j$. We searched \texttt{max\_bins} $\in\{32,256,1024\}$ (shape resolution) and \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $\in\{4,50\}$ (smoothness). CV AUC varied only between 0.7746 and 0.7757, so the EBM is robust to these settings. We selected 256 bins and leaves of at least 50 samples.

\subsubsection{Reference models}
A majority-class classifier gives the chance level. An untuned \texttt{HistGradient\allowbreak BoostingClassifier} serves as a black-box reference that measures what interpretability costs. Neither replaces the mandatory models.

\subsection{Interpretation}
Each model is interpreted with its own explanations, through InterpretML's global and local views. The decision tree is read through its structure, the rules extracted from its leaves and the impurity importance of each variable; a single prediction is explained by the decision path the client follows to its leaf. Logistic regression is read through its standardized coefficients; a single prediction is explained by each variable's contribution, coefficient $\times$ standardized value. The EBM is read through the shape function and the importance (mean absolute contribution) of each variable; a single prediction is explained by the value of each shape function for that client.
To compare \emph{which variables matter} for each model, we put the three importance measures side by side. Their units differ, so we divide each model's importances by their total: each value becomes the variable's share of that model's importance, and the rankings can be compared directly. For local explanations, we use the same four validation clients for all three models, with at least one from each class (Section~\ref{sec:local}).

The thresholds behave as theory predicts for calibrated models. $t_{\text{acc}}$ lies near 0.5 (0.47--0.49). $t_{\text{bal}}$ lies near the positive rate (0.18--0.23), because for a calibrated model balanced accuracy is maximized by flagging clients whose risk exceeds the base rate. \subsection{Standard classification metrics}
Once a cut-off is fixed, every client is either flagged or not, and the usual metrics apply. \emph{Accuracy} is the share of all clients classified correctly. \emph{Precision} is the share of flagged clients who really have difficulty, so it is high when there are few false alarms. \emph{Recall} (sensitivity) is the share of clients in difficulty who are flagged, so it is high when few cases are missed. The \emph{F1-score} combines precision and recall in a single number, which is useful when the classes are imbalanced. Table~\ref{tab:standard} reports them at both thresholds and Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the confusion matrices behind them.

\section{Models: interpretation and comparison}

\subsection{Decision tree: a six-rule risk profile}
The root split (Figure~\ref{fig:tree}) separates clients who are currently behind on payments (latest repayment status $\geq 1$; 22.8\,\% of clients, 43.8\,\% of whom have difficulty) from the rest (14.5\,\%). The second level splits both branches on almost the same payment-to-bill ratio (about 0.1), meaning ``paid less than about 10\,\% of the bills''. Together these two variables account for 85\,\% of the impurity reduction. Only 7 of the 15 variables are used at all; utilization, credit limit, bills, total payment, maximum delay, sex, education and marital status are ignored.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/tree_top_levels.pdf}
\caption{First three levels of the 17-leaf tree (status = latest repayment status, ratio = payment-to-bill ratio). Each node shows its share of clients and the class proportions [no difficulty, difficulty]; colour shows the majority class. The complete rule set is listed in Table~\ref{tab:rules}.}
\label{fig:tree}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{From tree to rule set}
At the operating threshold only the predicted class of each leaf matters, so sibling leaves that end in the same class can be merged without changing a single prediction. Collapsing such subtrees turns the 17 leaves into \textbf{9 rules, of which only 6 predict difficulty} (Table~\ref{tab:rules}). For example, the five leaves under ``behind on payments and paid at most 9.5\,\% of the bills'' all predict difficulty and merge into rule R1. The rule set reads: \emph{flag the client if any rule fires}. R1 alone covers 15\,\% of clients and captures 39\,\% of all difficulties (balanced accuracy 0.649 on the training set). Adding rules in order of risk raises balanced accuracy to 0.686 with four rules and 0.698 with all six, so a two- or three-rule version would trade a few points of recall for an explanation that fits on one line.

\subsection{Logistic regression: compact but constrained}
With standardized inputs, the largest coefficients (Figure~\ref{fig:lr}) belong to utilization ($+0.46$ log-odds per SD), the assistance plan ($+0.44$ against no plan, odds ratio 1.55), delayed months ($+0.38$), latest repayment status ($+0.37$) and log total payment ($-0.51$). The coefficients are stable: their range across the five CV fits is narrow, and none changes sign except near-zero education categories. Recorded sex has a small, consistent coefficient (male: $-0.07$).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figures/logit_coefficients.pdf}
\caption{Standardized LR coefficients (log-odds per standard deviation, or against the reference category: female, graduate school, married, no plan). Black segments show the range over the five CV refits.}
\label{fig:lr}
\end{figure}

Reading coefficients as effects is risky, however, because they are \emph{conditional} on all other, highly correlated, variables. Three coefficients have the opposite sign to the variable's marginal association with the target:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \var{maximum_repayment_delay_6m}: marginal correlation $+0.27$, coefficient $-0.08$. It is correlated 0.83 with delayed months.
\item \var{credit_limit}: marginal correlation $-0.12$, coefficient $+0.11$, given utilization and bills.
\item \var{payment_to_bill_ratio_6m}: marginal correlation $-0.14$, coefficient $+0.10$, given total payment.
\end{itemize}
LR does use the payment information, but it carries it mostly through log total payment and utilization rather than through the ratio. A second limitation is linearity. The model treats the undocumented negative repayment-status codes ($-2$, $-1$, $0$) as equally spaced points on a line, a meaning the data dictionary explicitly warns against inventing.

\subsection{EBM: the shape of each effect}
The EBM's shape functions (Figure~\ref{fig:ebm}) show the non-linear structure that the other two models flatten or approximate:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Payment-to-bill ratio} is the most important term, and its effect is \textbf{U-shaped}. Paying almost nothing raises the log-odds by up to $+0.7$, and risk is lowest for ratios of about 0.5--1.2 (empirical rate 8\,\%). Above about 1.7 the contribution jumps back to $+0.4$. This small group (2\,\% of clients, rate 22\,\%) corresponds to the catch-up payments and tiny or negative bills that the data dictionary describes.
\item \textbf{Repayment status} is flat across the non-positive codes and rises in steps from code 1 onwards. The model does not impose an order on the undocumented negative codes.
\item \textbf{Zero-payment months} are \emph{protective} at 1--2 months and harmful from 3 onwards, ending at $+0.84$ for six months. One missed payment does not mean the same as repeated ones, as the data dictionary anticipates. To test whether the dip is real, we edited the model: \texttt{monotonize} forces the term to be increasing. Applied inside each CV fold, this lowers the pooled out-of-fold AUC from 0.7756 to 0.7728, so the dip carries signal and we keep the learned shape.
\item \textbf{Age} is U-shaped: under 25 contributes $+0.58$ and over 57 about $+0.4$, while ages 30--55 are slightly protective.
\item \textbf{Utilization} increases almost linearly up to 1.0 and jumps for over-limit accounts above about 1.4 (rate 49\,\%).
\item The \textbf{assistance plan} adds $+0.41$ against $-0.11$ without a plan.
\end{itemize}
The bands ($\pm1$ SD over the EBM's bagged fits) are narrow wherever data are dense and wide in sparse tails (e.g.\ total payment above 130{,}000~NTD). Jumps in such regions should be read with caution.

\subsubsection{Where they agree}
All three models put recent repayment behaviour among their most important variables: delayed months, the latest repayment status and the assistance plan are in the top five of every model. The tree and the EBM rank the variables in almost the same order (rank correlation 0.86): latest repayment status, payment-to-bill ratio, delayed months, zero-payment months and the assistance plan form the top five of both. The tree and the EBM give little or no weight to education, marital status, average bill and sex; LR gives them small but non-zero weights (education is its sixth variable, with 4.5\,\%).

\subsubsection{Where they differ}
LR puts total payment (16.6\,\% of its importance) and utilization (15.1\,\%) first and ranks the payment-to-bill ratio only tenth (3.4\,\%), whereas the ratio is the first variable of the EBM (19.0\,\%) and the second of the tree (33.6\,\%). Its rank correlation with the other two is low (0.12 with the tree, 0.38 with the EBM). This does not mean that LR ignores how much clients pay: the ratio is computed from total payment and bills, so the same information can be taken from different variables. A linear model takes it from (log) total payment, while the tree and the EBM, which can bend their effects, use the ratio directly. This is an instance of the \emph{Rashomon effect}: when predictors carry overlapping information, several models fit the data almost equally well while relying on different variables, so a variable's importance is a property of the model, not of the data. Finally, the tree concentrates its importance (85\,\% on two variables, eight variables unused), while LR and the EBM spread it over many variables.

\subsubsection{Case A: correct, no difficulty}
The three models agree on the outcome but not fully on the reasons. The tree's path (status $\leq 0$, ratio between 0.105 and 1.2, no plan, age $\leq 24$) ends in a leaf with 11.8\,\% risk: being young is the only risk factor it notices. The EBM likewise adds $+0.56$ for the client's age of 23 and offsets it with the healthy payment ratio ($-0.42$). LR, being linear in age, adds only $+0.13$ for youth and instead credits low utilization and no delayed months. Here the two models with flexible effects agree against the linear one.

\subsubsection{Case B: correct, difficulty}
Here the explanations agree closely. The tree's path (status $\geq 1$, ratio $\leq 0.095$, few delayed and zero-payment months, plan = yes) ends in a leaf with 50.5\,\% risk. In LR and the EBM, two months of delay (status~2) is the main driver ($+0.66$ and $+0.72$), followed by the assistance plan (about $+0.4$ in both); the EBM also counts the low payment ratio as risk. Full utilization adds $+0.66$ in LR, a variable the tree does not use. This client sits right at the boundary (probabilities 0.47--0.53), which is typical of real difficulty cases and explains why recall is limited.

\subsubsection{Case C: conflict, the additive models are right}
This client is one month behind, has delayed four of six months, has a plan, and has a \emph{negative} average bill with a payment-to-bill ratio of 4.39. The tree's path (status $\geq 1$, ratio $> 0.095$, zero-payment months $\leq 3$, ratio $> 0.315$) leads to a low-risk leaf (15.4\,\%): for the tree a high ratio means a client who pays, so it misses the case. LR and the EBM both read the high ratio as \emph{risk} ($+0.72$ and $+0.48$) and add the four delayed months, which the tree does not use on this path. They correctly flag the client. The EBM's U-shaped ratio effect gives the right reading: a large ratio on a near-zero bill signals an irregular account, not a healthy one.

\subsubsection{Case D: conflict, the tree is right}
This client is up to date on every behavioural variable but has a September bill at twice the credit limit (utilization 2.0, the clipping value). Utilization dominates both additive explanations ($+1.80$ in LR, $+1.34$ in the EBM) and produces a false alarm. The tree never splits on utilization, so it predicts a low 0.06. The EBM's large over-limit contribution is learned from only 0.7\,\% of clients; for this client the tree's indifference happens to be correct.

These conflicts are informative rather than embarrassing. They show where each model's inductive bias matters: the tree's hard thresholds, LR's linearity and the EBM's freedom in sparse regions. They also show that a local explanation is only as trustworthy as the global shape behind it.

\subsection{Subgroup audit by recorded sex}
On the validation set at $t_{\text{bal}}$, the observed difficulty rates are 20.5\,\% (female, $n=3029$) and 22.3\,\% (male, $n=1971$). For all three models the gaps between groups in flag rate (at most 0.9 points), true-positive rate (1.8--2.1 points, slightly higher for women) and false-positive rate (0.2--0.8 points) are small. Sex is not used by the tree, has 2.4\,\% of LR's importance and is the least important of the EBM's fifteen terms (1.3\,\%). As the data dictionary stresses, similar error rates alone do not establish fairness; this is a descriptive check only.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Performance}
The three glass-box models are close (CV AUC 0.752--0.776), yet the ordering is consistent in every fold and on the validation set. The EBM gains most from its non-linear shapes for the payment ratio, zero-payment months and age, and LR gains about 0.012 AUC from the log transform of monetary variables, which is a manual version of the same idea. That the EBM \emph{outperforms} the untuned gradient-boosting reference supports the argument that, for tabular data with meaningful features, an interpretable model need not be less accurate. A tuned black box might gain a little, but the gap would have to justify losing a model whose every term can be inspected. Absolute performance is moderate (about 60\,\% of difficulties detected at a false-positive rate of about 21\,\% on the validation set), which reflects how much signal six months of behaviour contain rather than a weakness of any family.

\subsection{Interpretability}
The tree is the most \emph{simulatable} model: six rules can be applied by hand and explained to a client. It is also the coarsest: it outputs only 17 probability levels, ignores eight variables completely, and can be wrong in confidently explainable ways (case C). LR is compact, with one number per variable and stable signs across folds. It is the easiest model to state but the easiest to misread: its coefficients are conditional, three of them reverse the marginal sign, and linearity imposes an ordering on undocumented codes. The EBM requires reading fifteen curves, but each curve is an honest picture of what the model does. Its local explanations are exact sums of those curves, and its shapes surfaced the most domain-relevant insights of the project: the U-shaped ratio, the threshold in zero-payment months and the over-limit jump.

In our judgement the EBM offers the best balance. The tree's rule set remains the best \emph{communication} device and works as a sanity check on the EBM.

\subsection{Causality and the assistance plan}
All three models associate a repayment-assistance plan with higher risk (about $+0.4$ log-odds). Plans are assigned selectively, after the lender observes stress, so this is a marker of prior difficulty, not evidence that plans are harmful. The plan should not be used to deny support, and none of our explanations is causal.

\subsection{What could be improved}
Our 6-rule set is exact but still contains near-duplicate conditions: R4 and R6 differ only in age and the plan, and R3 and R5 share their first two conditions. A dedicated rule-list learner (e.g.\ CORELS or a greedy rule list from \texttt{imodels}) could produce an ordered list with fewer, shorter rules. For the EBM, allowing a few pairwise interactions (the tree suggests status~$\times$~ratio) would test whether the additive assumption costs accuracy. Our monotonization test shows that edits to the shape functions should be checked with CV rather than imposed by intuition, since the dip in zero-payment months looked implausible but was real; editing could still be justified for jumps in sparse tails (very old ages, high total payments), where the bands are wide.

For LR, binning or spline features for the payment ratio, repayment status and zero-payment months would let a linear model capture the shapes the EBM revealed, at the price of more coefficients. Finally, nested CV would remove the small optimism of selecting hyperparameters and thresholds on the same folds, although the validation results suggest this optimism is negligible here.

\section{Conclusion}
We built, tuned and interpreted a decision tree, logistic regression and an EBM under one CV protocol that covers model comparison, hyperparameters, a feature transformation and the classification threshold, and we confirmed the results on an untouched validation set. The EBM is the most accurate model (CV AUC 0.776, accuracy 0.817, balanced accuracy 0.705) and matches or beats a black-box reference. We therefore submit its predictions, refit on all labelled data with the accuracy-maximizing threshold 0.49; it flags 10.0\,\% of test clients and agrees with the other two families on 96--97\,\% of them.

The comparison showed that interpretability does not mean a single answer. The three models agree on the outcome for 87\,\% of clients and on the broad drivers (recent delays and low repayment). They differ on which correlated variable carries the signal and on the shape of its effect, and these differences explain the clients on which they disagree. Next steps are an ordered rule list, an EBM with selected interactions or monotone constraints, cost-sensitive thresholds agreed with domain experts, and a fuller fairness analysis that goes beyond descriptive error rates.
