\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Problem.} We predict \var{payment_difficulty_next_month}, a semi-synthetic indicator of whether a credit-card client has payment difficulty in the following month. The data derive from the \emph{Default of Credit Card Clients} data set \citep{yeh2009comparisons} and contain 25{,}000 labelled and 5{,}000 unlabelled clients described by 15 predictors: credit limit, four demographic fields (recorded sex, education, marital status, age), eight six-month behavioural summaries (repayment status and delays, bills, payments, the payment-to-bill ratio, zero-payment months, utilization) and whether the lender recorded a repayment-assistance plan. There are no missing values. The target is imbalanced: 21.2\,\% of clients are positive, so always predicting ``no difficulty'' already achieves 78.8\,\% accuracy.

\textbf{Strategy.} The goal is to understand \emph{what} each model learns and \emph{where explanations disagree}, as well as to predict well. We fit the three mandatory model families --- a decision tree, logistic regression (LR) and an Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) --- under one protocol: identical preprocessing logic, the same five cross-validation (CV) folds for every decision, the same hold-out set and the same four local instances. On top of each model's native explanation we add three model-agnostic comparisons: (i) permutation importance on a common scale, (ii) per-variable effect curves on the log-odds scale and (iii) additive log-odds decompositions of individual predictions.

\textbf{Main findings.}
(1)~The EBM is the best model on every metric. Its five-fold CV AUC of 0.776 exceeds LR (0.761) and the tree (0.752) in all five folds, and it also beats an untuned gradient-boosting black box (0.768), so interpretability costs nothing here.
(2)~The differences are modest in absolute terms (balanced accuracy 0.69--0.71 at a CV-chosen threshold), and the three models agree on 87\,\% of hold-out decisions.
(3)~The models agree that recent repayment behaviour --- the latest repayment status, the payment-to-bill ratio, and delayed and zero-payment months --- drives risk. They disagree on \emph{which} correlated variables carry this signal and on the \emph{shape} of the effects. The EBM finds strongly non-linear patterns: a U-shaped payment-ratio effect, a U-shaped age effect and a threshold in zero-payment months. LR cannot represent these, and the tree captures them only coarsely. These shape differences explain the local cases in which the models contradict each other.
(4)~We submit the EBM's predictions for \texttt{test.csv}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Validation protocol}
The labelled data are split once into an 80\,\% \emph{development} set (20{,}000 rows) and a 20\,\% \emph{hold-out} set (5{,}000 rows), stratified by the target with seed 411. All decisions --- model comparison, hyperparameters, feature transformations and classification thresholds --- use the \emph{same} five stratified folds of the development set, as the assignment requires. Because all models share the folds, differences can be checked fold by fold (paired comparison). The hold-out set is used once, after every choice is fixed, to confirm the CV estimates and to supply unseen instances for local explanation. The unlabelled test set is never used for fitting or selection. The submitted model is refit with its fixed settings on all 25{,}000 labelled rows.

\subsection{Selection criteria and threshold choice}
Hyperparameters are chosen by \textbf{CV ROC AUC}. AUC is threshold-free, so it separates \emph{how well a model ranks clients} from \emph{where the decision boundary is placed}. We deliberately do not use class weights. Instead, all three families produce unweighted, approximately calibrated probabilities, and the class imbalance is handled by choosing the threshold with CV. For each model we pool its out-of-fold probabilities and select two thresholds on a 0.01 grid:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item $t_{\text{acc}}$, which maximizes accuracy. It is used for the submitted test predictions, since the test set is graded on accuracy.
\item $t_{\text{bal}}$, which maximizes balanced accuracy, the mean of the two class recalls. It is our \emph{analysis operating point}, because missing a client in difficulty and raising a false alarm should both count. All class-specific results and local cases use $t_{\text{bal}}$.
\end{itemize}
For the tree and LR we also apply the \textbf{one-standard-error rule}: among configurations whose CV AUC lies within one standard error of the best, we keep the simplest (the shallowest tree, then the largest leaves; the strongest regularization). In an interpretability project we prefer the smaller model whenever the data cannot distinguish the two. This choice also corrects a flaw in our Phase~I submission, where the CV score came from a class-weighted search estimator that differed from the reported tree.

\subsection{The three classifiers}
\textbf{Decision tree} (\texttt{DecisionTreeClassifier}, CART; \citealp{breiman1984classification}). Categorical predictors are one-hot encoded; numerical predictors keep their original units, because splits are invariant to monotone transformations and raw thresholds are easier to read. We searched \texttt{max\_depth} $\in\{2,\dots,8,10\}$ and \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $\in\{25,50,100,200,400\}$, the two parameters that directly control how many rules a reader must follow. CV AUC rises up to depth~5 and then plateaus (Figure~\ref{fig:performance}, right). The best configuration (depth~5, leaves $\geq 25$; 32 leaves; AUC 0.755) and the one-SE choice (depth~5, leaves $\geq 400$; \textbf{17 leaves}; AUC 0.752) are statistically indistinguishable, so we keep the tree with half as many leaves. Deeper trees add leaves without gaining AUC.

\textbf{Logistic regression} (\texttt{LogisticRegression}, L2, \texttt{lbfgs}). Numerical predictors are standardized, so coefficients are comparable: each coefficient is the change in log-odds per standard deviation. Categorical predictors use reference (drop-first) coding, which removes the perfect collinearity of full one-hot encoding with the intercept. The four monetary variables are strongly right-skewed. Whether to apply a signed $\log(1+|x|)$ transform to them was a \emph{feature-transformation choice inside the CV grid}, crossed with $C\in\{0.001,\dots,100\}$. The transform raises CV AUC from 0.750 to 0.762 at every $C$. The one-SE rule selects $C=0.01$ (AUC 0.761), where the best $C=0.1$ gives 0.762.

\textbf{EBM} (\texttt{ExplainableBoostingClassifier}, InterpretML; \citealp{lou2012intelligible,nori2019interpretml}), with \texttt{interactions=0} so the model is a pure generalized additive model: $\operatorname{logit} P(y=1\mid x)=\beta_0+\sum_j f_j(x_j)$. Unlike the other two models, the EBM receives the raw columns. It bins numerical variables itself and treats the string columns as nominal, so each original variable gets exactly one shape function~$f_j$. We searched \texttt{max\_bins} $\in\{32,256,1024\}$ (shape resolution) and \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $\in\{4,50\}$ (smoothness). CV AUC varied only between 0.7746 and 0.7757, so the EBM is robust to these settings. We selected 256 bins and leaves of at least 50 samples.

\textbf{Reference models.} A majority-class classifier gives the chance level. An untuned \texttt{HistGradient\allowbreak BoostingClassifier} serves as a black-box reference that measures what interpretability costs. Neither replaces the mandatory models.

\subsection{Tools for comparing explanations}
The native explanations of the three families --- splits, coefficients and shape functions --- are not on a common scale, so we add three comparisons that are:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Permutation importance} \citep{breiman2001random}: the drop in hold-out AUC when one original variable is shuffled (10 repetitions), computed identically for every model.
\item \textbf{Effect curves}: for each model, the variable is set to each grid value in 1{,}000 development rows and the mean log-odds is recorded and centred (a partial-dependence curve on the log-odds scale; \citealp{molnar2022interpretable}). For the additive models this recovers their exact per-variable effect.
\item \textbf{Local log-odds decompositions}: for the EBM, the shape-function values; for LR, coefficient $\times$ standardized value (relative to an average client in the reference categories); for the tree, the change in node log-odds produced by each split on the decision path \citep{saabas2014interpreting}. All three add up to the model's log-odds, so the bars are directly comparable.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{All three models clearly beat chance}: they exceed the majority-class accuracy of 0.788 and have balanced accuracy near 0.70 against the chance level of 0.50. At $t_{\text{acc}}$ the models flag only about 10\,\% of clients and detect about 30\,\% of difficulties; at $t_{\text{bal}}$ they flag about 30\,\% and detect about 62\,\%.

\section{Models: interpretation and comparison}

\subsection{Decision tree: a six-rule risk profile}
The root split (Figure~\ref{fig:tree}) separates clients who are currently behind on payments (latest repayment status $\geq 1$; 22.8\,\% of clients, 43.8\,\% of whom have difficulty) from the rest (14.5\,\%). The second level splits both branches on almost the same payment-to-bill ratio (about 0.1), meaning ``paid less than about 10\,\% of the bills''. Together these two variables account for 85\,\% of the impurity reduction. Only 7 of the 15 variables are used at all; utilization, credit limit, bills, total payment, maximum delay, sex, education and marital status are ignored.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/tree_top_levels.pdf}
\caption{First three levels of the 17-leaf tree (status = latest repayment status, ratio = payment-to-bill ratio). Each node shows its share of clients and the class proportions [no difficulty, difficulty]; colour shows the majority class. The complete rule set is listed in Table~\ref{tab:rules}.}
\label{fig:tree}
\end{figure}

\textbf{From tree to rule set.} At the operating threshold only the predicted class of each leaf matters, so sibling leaves that end in the same class can be merged without changing a single prediction. Collapsing such subtrees turns the 17 leaves into \textbf{9 rules, of which only 6 predict difficulty} (Table~\ref{tab:rules}). For example, the five leaves under ``behind on payments and paid at most 9.5\,\% of the bills'' all predict difficulty and merge into rule R1. The rule set reads: \emph{flag the client if any rule fires}. R1 alone covers 15\,\% of clients and captures 39\,\% of all difficulties (development balanced accuracy 0.649). Adding rules in order of risk raises balanced accuracy to 0.686 with four rules and 0.698 with all six, so a two- or three-rule version would trade a few points of recall for an explanation that fits on one line.

\subsection{Logistic regression: compact but constrained}
With standardized inputs, the largest coefficients (Figure~\ref{fig:lr}) belong to utilization ($+0.46$ log-odds per SD), the assistance plan ($+0.44$ against no plan, odds ratio 1.55), delayed months ($+0.38$), latest repayment status ($+0.37$) and log total payment ($-0.51$). The coefficients are stable: their range across the five CV fits is narrow, and none changes sign except near-zero education categories. Recorded sex has a small, consistent coefficient (male: $-0.07$).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figures/logit_coefficients.pdf}
\caption{Standardized LR coefficients (log-odds per standard deviation, or against the reference category: female, graduate school, married, no plan). Black segments show the range over the five CV refits.}
\label{fig:lr}
\end{figure}

Reading coefficients as effects is risky, however, because they are \emph{conditional} on all other, highly correlated, variables. Three coefficients have the opposite sign to the variable's marginal association with the target:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \var{maximum_repayment_delay_6m}: marginal correlation $+0.27$, coefficient $-0.08$. It is correlated 0.83 with delayed months.
\item \var{credit_limit}: marginal correlation $-0.12$, coefficient $+0.11$, given utilization and bills.
\item \var{payment_to_bill_ratio_6m}: marginal correlation $-0.14$, coefficient $+0.10$, given total payment.
\end{itemize}
LR does use the payment information, but it carries it mostly through log total payment and utilization rather than through the ratio. A second limitation is linearity. The model treats the undocumented negative repayment-status codes ($-2$, $-1$, $0$) as equally spaced points on a line, a meaning the data dictionary explicitly warns against inventing.

\subsection{EBM: the shape of each effect}
The EBM's shape functions (Figure~\ref{fig:ebm}) show the non-linear structure that the other two models flatten or approximate:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Payment-to-bill ratio} is the most important term, and its effect is \textbf{U-shaped}. Paying almost nothing raises the log-odds by up to $+0.7$, and risk is lowest for ratios of about 0.5--1.2 (empirical rate 8\,\%). Above about 1.7 the contribution jumps back to $+0.4$. This small group (2\,\% of clients, rate 22\,\%) corresponds to the catch-up payments and tiny or negative bills that the data dictionary describes.
\item \textbf{Repayment status} is flat across the non-positive codes and rises in steps from code 1 onwards. The model does not impose an order on the undocumented negative codes.
\item \textbf{Zero-payment months} are \emph{protective} at 1--2 months and harmful from 3 onwards, ending at $+0.84$ for six months. One missed payment does not mean the same as repeated ones, as the data dictionary anticipates.
\item \textbf{Age} is U-shaped: under 25 contributes $+0.58$ and over 57 about $+0.4$, while ages 30--55 are slightly protective.
\item \textbf{Utilization} increases almost linearly up to 1.0 and jumps for over-limit accounts above about 1.4 (rate 49\,\%).
\item The \textbf{assistance plan} adds $+0.41$ against $-0.11$ without a plan.
\end{itemize}
The bands ($\pm1$ SD over the EBM's bagged fits) are narrow wherever data are dense and wide in sparse tails (e.g.\ total payment above 130{,}000~NTD). Jumps in such regions should be read with caution.

\subsection{Global comparison: same signal, different carriers and shapes}
\textbf{Which variables matter?} Native importances are not comparable across families, so Figure~\ref{fig:perm} measures every model the same way. The three models agree that recent repayment behaviour dominates, and that education, marital status, average bill and sex are irrelevant (permutation importance $\approx 0$). They \emph{disagree on which correlated variable carries the payment signal}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item The tree routes almost everything through the payment-to-bill ratio (AUC drop 0.122) and repayment status (0.057).
\item LR relies on total payment (0.073) and utilization (0.056) and barely uses the ratio (0.004).
\item The EBM spreads importance across seven variables, none exceeding 0.032.
\end{itemize}
The rank correlations of the importances are 0.85 (tree--EBM), 0.73 (LR--EBM) and only 0.36 (tree--LR). This is a clear instance of the \emph{Rashomon effect}: several models of similar accuracy tell different stories because the payment variables are partly redundant (the ratio is a function of total payment and bills). A variable's importance is therefore a property of the model, not of the data. The EBM's smaller drops do not mean that it uses weaker signals: when one of its many inputs is shuffled, the others partly compensate.

\textbf{How do the variables act?} Figure~\ref{fig:effects} overlays the three models' effect curves for the four most important numerical variables:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Payment-to-bill ratio}: the tree and the EBM agree on the steep fall from 0 to about 0.5, but beyond 1.2 the tree is flat while the EBM rises again. LR fits a single, slightly \emph{increasing} line, which contradicts both of the other models over the range where most clients lie.
\item \textbf{Repayment status}: all three agree on the step at code~1. LR alone extrapolates the line into the negative codes.
\item \textbf{Delayed months}: LR is linear, the EBM is convex, and the tree is nearly flat, because its splits already use repayment status, which is correlated 0.64 with delayed months.
\item \textbf{Zero-payment months}: the EBM's dip at 1--2 months is invisible to the other two.
\end{itemize}
The tree's effects are coarse step functions; LR's are straight lines by construction. The EBM shows these shapes because its additive structure lets the data choose each curve, which is also why it is the most accurate of the three.

\textbf{Case A (correct, no difficulty).} The three models agree on the outcome but not fully on the reasons. LR, being linear in age, adds only $+0.13$ for youth and instead credits low utilization and no delayed months. Here two models with flexible effects agree against the linear one.

\textbf{Case B (correct, difficulty).} Here the explanations agree closely. Two months of delay (status~2) is the main driver in every model ($+0.66$ to $+1.06$), followed by the assistance plan (about $+0.4$ in all three). A low payment ratio contributes in the tree and the EBM. Full utilization adds $+0.66$ in LR, which the tree ignores. This client sits right at the boundary (probabilities 0.47--0.53), which is typical of real difficulty cases and explains why recall is limited.

\textbf{Case C (conflict; the additive models are right).} This client is one month behind, has delayed four of six months, has a plan, and has a \emph{negative} average bill with a payment-to-bill ratio of 4.39. The tree follows the branch ``status $\geq1$ and ratio $>0.095$'' and treats the high ratio as strongly protective ($-1.25$), so it predicts 0.15 and misses the case. LR and the EBM both read the high ratio as \emph{risk} ($+0.72$ and $+0.48$) and add the four delayed months, which the tree does not use on this path. They correctly flag the client. The EBM's U-shaped ratio effect gives the right reading: a large ratio on a near-zero bill signals an irregular account, not a healthy one.

\textbf{Case D (conflict; the tree is right).} This client is up to date on every behavioural variable but has a September bill at twice the credit limit (utilization 2.0, the clipping value). Utilization dominates both additive explanations ($+1.80$ in LR, $+1.34$ in the EBM) and produces a false alarm. The tree never splits on utilization, so it predicts a low 0.06. The EBM's large over-limit contribution is learned from only 0.7\,\% of clients; for this client the tree's indifference happens to be correct.

These conflicts are informative rather than embarrassing. They show where each model's inductive bias matters: the tree's hard thresholds, LR's linearity and the EBM's freedom in sparse regions. They also show that a local explanation is only as trustworthy as the global shape behind it.

\subsection{Subgroup audit by recorded sex}
On the hold-out set at $t_{\text{bal}}$, the observed difficulty rates are 20.5\,\% (female, $n=3029$) and 22.3\,\% (male, $n=1971$). For all three models the gaps between groups in flag rate (at most 0.9 points), true-positive rate (1.8--2.1 points, slightly higher for women) and false-positive rate (0.2--0.8 points) are small. Sex has zero permutation importance in every model, and its EBM term is the least important of the fifteen. As the data dictionary stresses, similar error rates alone do not establish fairness; this is a descriptive check only.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Performance.} The three glass-box models are close (CV AUC 0.752--0.776), yet the ordering is consistent in every fold and on the hold-out set. The EBM gains most from its non-linear shapes for the payment ratio, zero-payment months and age, and LR gains about 0.012 AUC from the log transform of monetary variables, which is a manual version of the same idea. That the EBM \emph{outperforms} the untuned gradient-boosting reference supports the argument that, for tabular data with meaningful features, an interpretable model need not be less accurate \citep{rudin2019stop,caruana2015intelligible}. A tuned black box might gain a little, but the gap would have to justify losing a model whose every term can be inspected. Absolute performance is moderate (about 60\,\% of difficulties detected at a false-positive rate of about 21\,\% on the hold-out set), which reflects how much signal six months of behaviour contain rather than a weakness of any family.

\textbf{Interpretability (subjective).}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \emph{The tree} is the most \emph{simulatable} model: six rules can be applied by hand and explained to a client. It is also the coarsest. It outputs only 17 probability levels, ignores eight variables completely, and can be wrong in confidently explainable ways (case C).
\item \emph{LR} is compact, with one number per variable and stable signs across folds. It is the easiest model to state but the easiest to misread: its coefficients are conditional, three of them reverse the marginal sign, and linearity imposes an ordering on undocumented codes.
\item \emph{The EBM} requires reading fifteen curves, but each curve is an honest picture of what the model does. Local explanations are exact sums of those curves, and the shapes surfaced the most domain-relevant insights of the project (the U-shaped ratio, the threshold in zero-payment months, the over-limit jump).
\end{itemize}
In our judgement the EBM offers the best balance. The tree's rule set remains the best \emph{communication} device and works as a sanity check on the EBM.

\textbf{Causality and the assistance plan.} All three models associate a repayment-assistance plan with higher risk (about $+0.4$ log-odds). Plans are assigned selectively, after the lender observes stress, so this is a marker of prior difficulty, not evidence that plans are harmful. The plan should not be used to deny support, and none of our explanations is causal.

\textbf{What could be improved.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \emph{Rule list}: our 6-rule set is exact but still contains near-duplicate conditions. For example, R4 and R6 differ only in age and the plan, and R3 and R5 share their first two conditions. A dedicated rule-list learner (e.g.\ CORELS or a greedy rule list from \texttt{imodels}) could produce an ordered list with fewer, shorter rules.
\item \emph{EBM}: allowing a few pairwise interactions (the tree suggests status~$\times$~ratio) would test whether the additive assumption costs accuracy. Monotonicity constraints or manual editing of shape functions could remove implausible jumps in sparse tails (very old ages, high total payments).
\item \emph{LR}: binning or spline features for the payment ratio, repayment status and zero-payment months would let a linear model capture the shapes the EBM revealed, at the price of more coefficients.
\item \emph{Protocol}: nested CV would remove the small optimism of selecting hyperparameters and thresholds on the same folds. The hold-out results suggest this optimism is negligible here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We built, tuned and interpreted a decision tree, logistic regression and an EBM under one CV protocol that covers model comparison, hyperparameters, a feature transformation and the classification threshold, and we confirmed the results on an untouched hold-out set. The EBM is the most accurate model (CV AUC 0.776, accuracy 0.817, balanced accuracy 0.705) and matches or beats a black-box reference. We therefore submit its predictions, refit on all labelled data with the accuracy-maximizing threshold 0.49; it flags 10.0\,\% of test clients and agrees with the other two families on 96--97\,\% of them.

The comparison showed that interpretability does not mean a single answer. The three models agree on the outcome for 87\,\% of clients and on the broad drivers (recent delays and low repayment). They differ on which correlated variable carries the signal and on the shape of its effect, and these differences explain the clients on which they disagree. Next steps are an ordered rule list, an EBM with selected interactions or monotone constraints, cost-sensitive thresholds agreed with domain experts, and a fuller fairness analysis that goes beyond descriptive error rates.
